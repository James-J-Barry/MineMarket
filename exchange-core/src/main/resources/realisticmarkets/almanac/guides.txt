@money_and_dealer Money & the Dealer
Every Basic Exchange in this world trades with the same Dealer. The Dealer buys what you bring and sells what you need, and it pays in dollars: dimes, $1, $10 and $100 bills.

Dollars are real items. You can't craft them, mobs don't drop them, and if you die carrying them they fall to the ground like anything else. Keep what you don't need in a chest.

The Sell tab shows three prices per item. Normal is the Dealer's published fair value, as of yesterday's close. Market is where the Dealer's price sits right now. Pays is what you get for the next item. Your first sale went through close to Normal because the Dealer had none of that item in stock.

If the Dealer won't buy something, no market exists for it yet. More markets open as you unlock new tools here at the Almanac.

Fair values move every day, even when nobody trades. They wander, follow trends that can last a week or two, and jump on news, and nothing pulls them back where they started except very slowly. So Normal is a day old by the time you read it: a guide, not a promise.

Real world: money exists so you don't have to find someone who wants your wheat and also happens to have the pickaxe you need. Economists call that the double coincidence of wants.

@spread The Spread
You just bought an item and sold the same item straight back, and you ended up with less money than you started with. That gap is the spread.

The Dealer always quotes two prices. The ask is what it charges when you buy. The bid is what it pays when you sell. At the Basic Exchange they sit 20% apart, centered on the market price. For wheat at $0.50 the ask is $0.55 and the bid is $0.45, so a round trip costs you $0.10 per wheat.

Why does the Dealer get that cut? It stands ready to trade any time, in any amount, and it carries the risk of holding stock it may have to sell for less later. The spread is its pay for that service.

Spreads differ by item. Components like Brass Fittings trade at a 40% spread, because the Dealer can't easily resell spare parts. Buy them when you need them, not to hold.

The Merchant License, now available in Upgrades, cuts your spread from 20% to 12% on every trade. The more you trade, the faster it pays for itself.

Real world: an airport currency booth quotes one rate to sell you euros and a worse one to buy them back. The difference is its spread.

@price_impact Price Impact
You sold 256 of one item in a single day, and each one paid a little less than the one before.

The Dealer lowers its price as its stock grows. Every item has a depth: roughly how much the Dealer can absorb before its price falls to about a third of normal. Wheat's depth is 256. The price moves unit by unit inside one sale, so a big sale pays the average price along the way, not the price at the start.

Here is wheat, sold to a Dealer that has none in stock:

64 wheat pays $25.48, not the $28.80 you'd get at the first bid.
256 wheat pays $72.82, not $115.20.
1,024 wheat pays $113.09.

No amount of wheat will ever pay more than $115.20 in one go, because the last units are worth almost nothing. After the 256-wheat sale the bid has fallen from $0.45 to $0.17.

So a bigger farm doesn't earn proportionally more. Selling a little at a time, or selling different things, does.

Buying works the same way in reverse: buy a lot at once and the ask climbs as you go.

Real world: when a big fund dumps a large block of shares at once, the price slides as it sells. Traders call this market impact, and large sellers split their orders to limit it.

@recovery Recovery & Patience
You pushed a price down, waited, and sold again once it came back. That's the Dealer's recovery at work.

Between your visits the Dealer sells its stock on to the outside world. Its inventory of each item shrinks by about 40% every in-game day, and the price climbs back toward normal as it does.

Take 256 wheat sold at once. Right after, the Market price sits at 37% of Normal. Then:

After 2 days: back to about 69%.
After 4 days: about 87%.
After about 5 days: past 90%.

The steady pace a Dealer can absorb is about half its depth per day. For wheat that's roughly 128 a day, worth about $21. Sell faster and you mostly sell into your own price drop. Sell at that pace and every batch goes through near full price.

So patience is a strategy. Store goods in a chest, check the Market price, and sell when it has recovered. Rotate between items while each one recovers.

Not all of the drop comes back. Every 256 wheat sold also lowers Normal itself by about 1%, for good: the world simply has more wheat. And waiting has a cost. Goods in a chest earn nothing, money in a Bank Vault earns interest, and Normal may move against you while you wait.

Real world: after a big trade, market makers slowly sell off the inventory they took on and prices drift back. Traders call this liquidity recovering.

@diversification Diversification
You earned $20 from each of three different groups of goods in one day. That beats selling the same amount of one thing.

Each item has its own Dealer price and its own depth. Selling wheat does nothing to the price of iron. So the first units of every item sell near full price, while the hundredth unit of any one item sells well below it.

Compare two ways to sell 192 items, all with wheat's depth of 256:

192 of one item at once keeps about 70% of its full value.
64 each of three items keeps about 88%.

Same work, about a quarter more money, just from spreading it out. The best income comes from several small operations that each sell near their steady pace, not one giant farm.

Diversification also protects you. Fair values move every day, trend and jump on news, and any single item can have a bad week. If you sell many things, one weak price hurts less.

There is a limit. Spreading into goods that take ages to gather, or are worth very little, can cost more time than it earns. Diversify across things that each pay their way.

Real world: investors spread their money across many companies so one bad result can't sink them. The old saying is don't put all your eggs in one basket.

@cash_on_hand Cash on Hand
You bought a Bill Clip. It holds up to nine stacks of bills and coins, and every exchange pays from it and pays into it, so you no longer dig through your inventory for change.

Cash is convenient, but it's also fragile. A Bill Clip is an item: drop it, or die carrying it, and the money inside goes with it. The more you carry, the more you can lose in one bad night.

Every trade also leaks a little. The Dealer rounds in its own favor: sales round down to the dime, purchases round up. Sell 64 wheat and the exact price is $25.48, but you're paid $25.40. Eight cents is nothing once. Across hundreds of trades it adds up, and it's the same for everyone who deals in cash.

So carry what you need to trade today and keep the rest somewhere safe. Later, a Bank Vault will hold money you can't lose by falling in lava, and it will even pay interest.

Real world: cash in your pocket is instantly spendable but can be lost or stolen; money in a bank is safer but takes a trip to reach. People balance the two, and so will you.

@reading_a_quote Reading a Quote
You put up a Price Board. Each item on it shows two prices: the Bid, what the Dealer pays you, and the Ask, what it charges you. The arrow compares the market price with Normal: up means the Dealer is paying above normal right now, down means below.

A quote is only good for the next item. Wheat shows a bid of $0.45, but sell 64 at once and the last ones fetch less. Right after that sale the board shows a bid of $0.35. Wait a few days and it climbs back.

So read a board like a trader. A down arrow on something you hold means wait if you can. An up arrow means sell now, before someone else, or the market, takes it away. Compare items side by side to decide what to sell today.

The board shows the public price at the Basic Exchange. If you hold a Merchant License, your own prices are a little better than what the board says.

Real world: a stock quote shows a bid and an ask, and they're good only for a limited number of shares. Anyone trading a large amount knows the price will move against them.

@transaction_costs Transaction Costs
You bought the Merchant License. Your spread at the Basic Exchange drops from 20% to 12%, for good.

Here's what that means in dollars. Wheat's bid rises from $0.45 to $0.47 and its ask falls from $0.55 to $0.53. Sell 64 wheat and you're paid $26.60 instead of $25.40. Buying gets about 4% cheaper too.

That's a small gain per trade against a big fixed cost. The license cost $150, and it raises your sale proceeds by about 4.4%. To earn the $150 back from selling alone, you need to sell about $3,500 worth of goods. A casual trader may never get there. A busy one gets there in a few days and profits from then on.

That's the difference between a fixed cost and a variable cost. The spread is a variable cost: you pay it on every trade. The license is a fixed cost: you pay it once. The more you trade, the more a fixed cost is worth paying to cut the variable one.

The license only applies at the Basic Exchange. The Capital's buyers charge their own spread.

Real world: traders who buy a seat on an exchange, or a flat-fee brokerage plan, pay a large fixed cost to cut what every trade costs them. It only pays off if they trade a lot.

@two_markets Two Markets
You bought a Trade Route Crate. Goods you load into it travel to the Capital, a second market with its own prices, and are sold there when they arrive a day later, minus 5% freight.

The Capital pays more than the local Dealer for some things and less for others. Farm goods are worth 30% more there; mining goods 10% less. Its spread is 15%.

Take 128 wheat. Sold locally, it pays $45.30. Shipped, the Capital pays $60.50, freight takes $3.10, and you receive $57.40, about a quarter more. Iron goes the other way: 16 ingots pay $110.50 at home but only $97.00 after shipping.

Can you buy wheat here and ship it for a profit? Not in bulk. Buying 64 wheat costs $40.00 and shipping it returns $32.30. The spreads, the freight and your own price impact eat the gap.

The price you get is the Capital's price on arrival, not when you ship. If others' goods, or your own last shipment, arrived first, it will be lower. That delay is settlement risk.

Real world: grain has always sold for more in the city than on the farm. Merchants profit from the difference only when it's bigger than the cost of moving goods between the two markets.

@interest_and_compounding Interest and Compounding
You opened a Bank Vault. Money inside it is safe: it doesn't drop when you die, and nobody else can open your vault. It also earns interest at the central bank's rate: 0.3% a day to begin with, and the bank reviews it every week, so it can rise or fall.

That sounds tiny, but the interest itself earns interest. Put in $1,000 and after one day you have $1,003.00. The next day you earn 0.3% of $1,003, not of $1,000. After a week you have $1,021.10. After 30 days you have $1,094.00, where plain interest on the original $1,000 would have paid only $90. Leave it alone and the money doubles by day 232.

The bank credits interest in whole dimes. The fractions carry over to the next day, so nothing is lost; you just see it a dime at a time.

Prices creep up too, about 0.1% a day, so cash in a chest slowly buys less. In the vault it still grows faster than prices. Keep it in perspective, though: $1,000 earns about $3 a day, while a good farm earns $20 or more.

To see your balance you need your Passbook in the vault's slot. The vault can only be broken once it's empty, so a vault is a place, not something you carry.

Real world: a savings account pays interest on interest in the same way. Over years that small daily edge is what turns steady saving into real wealth.

@term_and_liquidity Term and Liquidity
You unlocked Certificates of Deposit. A CD locks money away for a fixed term in exchange for a better rate.

Compare $500 for a week at the starting rates. Left in the vault it grows to $510.50. In a 7-day CD at 0.45% a day it pays $515.90. The longer CD pays more still: $1,000 for 21 days at 0.60% a day becomes $1,133.80, against $1,064.90 in the vault.

CD rates move with the central bank's rate, but a CD keeps the rate it was issued at. Why pay more for longer? Because you give something up: the right to use that money whenever you like. Being able to spend money right away is called liquidity, and it has a price. Cash in a CD early and you get your principal back but none of the interest, so you'd have done better leaving it in the vault.

So match the term to your plans. Money you'll need for the next upgrade belongs in the vault. Money you won't touch for three weeks can earn more in a long CD.

A CD is also a paper you carry. Whoever holds it can cash it, so losing it means losing the money, and each certificate can only be redeemed once.

Real world: banks pay more on term deposits than on savings accounts because they can lend that money out for longer. Tying money up is a cost, and the higher rate is your payment for it.

@leverage_and_collateral Leverage and Collateral
The bank will lend against your goods, but not at their market value. Here is what each pile can borrow:

15 diamonds (market $1,500): $775.90 at 0.75% a day
256 iron (market $2,048): $651.50 at 1.00% a day
64 gold (market $960): $454.00 at 0.81% a day
1,500 oak logs (market $1,500): $110.20 at 1.31% a day

The bank asks what it would get if it had to dump everything on the Dealer today, then takes a safety cut: 10% for gems and gold, 20% for other metals, 40% for crops and logs. You can borrow 80% of what's left. Cheap, bulky goods crash the price as they're sold, so a mountain of logs backs almost nothing, and the rate is higher too.

Borrowing lets you do more with what you own. That's leverage: gains grow, and so do losses.

Every dawn the bank re-values your collateral. It must cover at least 110% of what you owe. Borrow $700 against those diamonds and a fall of about a fifth in the diamond price triggers a margin call. You then have one day to add collateral or repay. After that the bank sells your goods itself, cheapest first, at whatever the Dealer pays that morning.

Real world: lenders value collateral at fire-sale prices, not market prices, and margin calls force sales at the worst moment. Leverage wiped out many traders in 1929.

@order_books Order Books
You unlocked the Trading Floor. Unlike the Dealer, the Floor doesn't set prices. It collects orders from buyers and sellers, NPC traders and you, into an order book, one for each item, and every ten seconds it runs an auction.

Take a wheat book with three buyers and three sellers:

Buy 20 at $0.52
Buy 30 at $0.50
Buy 40 at $0.48
Sell 25 at $0.47
Sell 30 at $0.49
Sell 50 at $0.51

The auction looks for the price where the most wheat changes hands. At $0.50 the two top buyers want 50 wheat and the two cheapest sellers offer 55, so 50 trade. No price trades more.

Everyone who trades gets that one price. The buyer who would have paid $0.52 pays $0.50, and the seller who would have taken $0.47 gets $0.50. The seller at $0.49 sells 25 of their 30; the other 5 wait for the next auction. The buyer at $0.48 and the seller at $0.51 don't trade at all.

Nobody on the Floor owes you a trade. If no one wants your wheat at your price, it doesn't sell. The Dealer always buys, just at a worse price the more you sell.

Real world: stock exchanges open and close each day with this kind of auction, one price that matches as many buyers and sellers as possible.

@limit_and_market_orders Limit and Market Orders
Every order costs one Order Slip. It's either a limit order or a market order.

A limit order names your worst price: the most you'll pay, or the least you'll take. It trades only at that price or better, and it waits in the book, auction after auction, until it fills, you cancel it, or dawn comes. Limit orders are day orders: whatever hasn't filled by the next dawn expires, and your goods or cash come back.

A market order names no price. It joins the next auction only and takes whatever price clears. So that it can trade, the Floor lets it reach 50% past the last price. A market buy of 16 wheat when wheat last traded at $0.50 sets aside 16 x $0.75 = $12.00, and a market sell will accept as little as $0.25. Whatever doesn't fill comes straight back, with the cash you didn't spend.

Changed your mind? The Floor screen can move an open order to a new price without another slip. Either way the Floor holds your goods or cash from the moment you place the order. Fills, refunds and a Trade Receipt showing the average price wait at any Trading Floor.

Use a limit when the price matters most, and a market order when time does.

Real world: a market order is the fastest way to trade, but in a thin or fast market it can fill far from the last price you saw. That's why professionals mostly use limits.

@liquidity_and_market_makers Liquidity and Market Makers
Every Floor book has a market maker, an NPC who always offers to buy a little below the price and sell a little above it. With wheat at $0.50 it bids $0.49 and asks $0.51, a 4% spread where the Dealer's is 20%. Diamonds at $100 get $98.50 and $101.50.

The maker earns the spread on every pair of trades, as long as buyers and sellers arrive in equal numbers. When they don't, it protects itself. Sell it wheat until it holds twice what it wants and it drops its quotes to $0.47 and $0.50, to buy less and sell more. Buy it out and it raises them to $0.49 and $0.53.

That's liquidity: how much you can trade without moving the price. In a deep book like wheat's the maker quotes 32 at a time and other traders add more, so a stack or two barely moves it. In a thin book like diamonds' the maker quotes 3 at a time, and every trade moves the price.

The NPCs also run out. A big seller drains their cash, and they take about two days to recover, like the Dealer's stock.

Check the bid and ask before you trade. If they're wide, the book is thin or was just hit hard: use a limit order.

Real world: market makers are paid through the spread for standing ready to trade. In a panic they widen their quotes or step away, and liquidity vanishes when people need it most.

@news_and_markets News and Markets
You built a Newsstand. Its board carries the market news as it breaks each dawn: a drought, a bumper harvest, a mine collapse, and which goods each story should push up or down.

News takes time to spread. When the board goes up, most traders and the Dealer haven't heard yet, so for a while prices still ignore it. The sooner you act, the better your price. Wait too long and the move has already happened.

Read about a drought early and you can buy wheat, carrots and potatoes at the old price. Once everyone knows, prices jump, then fade over the following days as the shock passes; a small part of the move stays for good. Sell into the jump, not after it has faded.

Bad news works too. A bumper harvest means farm goods will fall, so sell what you hold before the rest of the market catches on.

Headlines say which way, not how far. The same kind of story can move prices half as much as last time, or half as much again. Don't bet everything on one story, and remember the books are shallow: buying a lot moves the price against you.

Real world: news moves prices within seconds today, so traders pay to hear it first. Before the telegraph, whoever carried news from a battle could make a fortune before anyone else knew.

@reading_a_chart Reading a Chart
You built a Ticker Tape. Pick any Floor book on it and it draws that book's last seven days of trading, four points a day, and keeps the chart up to date while you watch.

Each point is where the price closed in that quarter of a day, with a thin bar from its high to its low. A long bar means the price swung around; a short one means a calm market. The bars along the bottom show how much traded: a price move on heavy trading means more than one on a handful of items.

Look for three things. The trend: are the closes climbing or sliding over the week? Jumps: a sudden step up or down, often news reaching the market. Did it hold, or fade over the next days? And the range: the gap between the week's high and low tells you how far this book usually moves, so you know what a normal day looks like.

Charts show what happened, not what will. A trend can keep going or stop dead. Use a chart to judge how risky a trade is, not to promise yourself a profit.

Real world: traders have read prices off paper strips since ticker machines printed every trade in the 1870s. A chart shows a market's mood, but no pattern is a promise.

@owning_a_share Owning a Share
You opened a Stock Exchange. A share is a small piece of a company, and the company's earnings belong to the people who own it.

Every quarter, seven in-game days, each company reports what it earned and pays part of it to every share as a dividend. Overworld Utility & Light starts out earning about $1.14 a share each quarter and pays $1.02 of it. The rest stays in the company and helps it grow, so the share is worth more.

Your shares are Share Certificates: papers for 1, 10 or 100 shares. Whoever holds a certificate owns it, so keep them safe; lose one and those shares are gone. Dividends wait on the paper until you bring it to a Stock Exchange and collect. Shares the exchange holds for you, in an open order or waiting to be collected, earn their dividend too.

To get a quarter's dividend you must own the share when the quarter ends. Once it's paid the company has that much less cash, so the price drops by about the dividend. Buying just before and selling just after gains you nothing.

Real world: companies first sold shares so that many people could fund one venture and share its profits. For centuries the proof of ownership was a paper certificate, and dividends were the reason to hold it.

@valuing_a_company Valuing a Company
What is a share worth? Whatever the company will earn for its owners from now on, counted in today's money.

The Company tab shows the last four quarters: revenue, costs and earnings. Watch the costs. Much of what a company spends is fixed, so its earnings swing far more than its sales. Deepslate Mining sells iron, gold and diamonds. If iron falls 20%, its sales fall about 12% but its earnings fall about 37%.

Two numbers help you compare companies. Price to earnings (P/E) is the share price divided by a year of earnings, four quarters. At the start Deepslate Mining trades at about 11.5 times earnings and Overworld Utility at about 16: investors pay more for steady earnings. Dividend yield is a year of dividends divided by the price, about 5.6% for the utility.

A low P/E can mean a bargain or a company in trouble. A high one can mean fast growth or too much hope. And earnings react to what you do: flood the iron market and the miner's next report will show it.

Real world: Benjamin Graham taught investors to compare a price with what the business earns and to buy with a margin of safety. In the short run the market is a voting machine; in the long run, a weighing machine.

@risk_and_return Risk and Return
You collected your first dividend. How do shares compare with everything else you could do with money?

Prices in this world creep up about 0.1% a day, so cash in a chest loses that much buying power every day. Goods roughly keep pace with prices, but they swing with every harvest and strike. The vault pays the central bank's rate, 0.3% a day to begin with, about 0.2% more than prices rise, with no risk at all.

Shares should earn more than the vault, because owners take the risk. Investors here expect between 0.25% and 0.55% a day above rising prices, depending on the company: the least for the steady utility, the most for Redstone Dynamics, whose price can double or halve in a few weeks. That extra is the price of risk.

One company can have a terrible stretch. A spread of several is far calmer, because their bad quarters rarely line up. Diversifying gives up little of the return and removes much of the risk.

So keep money you'll need soon in the vault, and put money you won't touch for weeks into a spread of shares.

Real world: over the last century stocks have beaten bank deposits by a few percent a year, with deep crashes along the way. That gap is called the equity risk premium.

@custody Custody
Your papers are bearer papers: whoever holds a Share Certificate or a CD owns it. The exchange doesn't know your name. Die with a stack of certificates in your pocket and whoever picks them up owns those shares; drop them in lava and they're simply gone.

A Portfolio Binder holds 27 papers in one item and, when you open it, adds up what they're all worth at today's prices. The papers themselves never show a value, so the binder is how you see your whole portfolio at once. It is still one item you can lose, so don't carry more in it than you need.

A Safe Deposit Box is for the rest. It holds 54 slots of papers and money, explosions can't touch it, only you can open it, and it can't be broken while anything is inside. Keep long-term holdings there and carry only what you're about to trade or present.

Shares in the box still earn their dividends. Bring the certificates to a Stock Exchange whenever it suits you and everything that built up is paid.

Real world: investors once kept paper stock certificates in bank safe deposit boxes. Today almost all shares are held electronically by brokers and custodians, so nobody loses a fortune with a piece of paper.

@bonds_and_yield Bonds and Yield
You opened a Bond Desk. A bond is a loan you make: you hand over $100 today, the borrower pays you a coupon every quarter, and at maturity you get your $100 back.

At 0.3% a day a bond's coupon is $2.11 a quarter. Hold a 4-quarter bond to the end and it pays four coupons and the $100: $108.44 for your $100. That extra is its yield, the return you lock in when you buy.

The Treasury borrows for the central bank and always pays. Companies borrow too, and pay more, because they might not.

Longer bonds pay a little more to make up for tying your money up. The Buy tab shows it as the yield curve: at the start a 2-quarter Treasury yields 0.308% a day and an 8-quarter one 0.332%.

Coupons wait on the paper until you bring it to the Bond Desk, like dividends. Bonds are bearer papers: whoever holds one gets paid, so store them safely.

You don't have to hold to the end. The desk buys bonds back any time, at today's price, which isn't always what you paid.

Real world: governments and companies borrow most of their money this way, and the bond market is bigger than the stock market.

@interest_rate_risk Interest Rate Risk
Your bond pays a fixed coupon. Rates don't stay fixed.

Say you bought an 8-quarter bond at the going rate, and a week later the central bank raises its rate by 0.05% a day. New bonds now pay more than yours, so nobody will pay full price for it: its price falls about 2.6%. A 2-quarter bond loses only 0.7%, because its money comes back sooner. When the central bank cuts, the same bonds rise by about as much.

So bond prices move opposite to interest rates, and the further away a bond's money is, the more it moves. Traders call that distance duration.

None of this matters if you hold to maturity: you still get every coupon and your $100 back. It matters if you need to sell early, and it's an opportunity if you read the rate news first. The Newsstand reports each decision, and the rest of the market takes a while to react.

Short bonds are a safe place for money you'll need soon. Long bonds pay a bit more, and swing more.

Real world: in 2022 central banks raised rates fast, and long-dated government bonds, supposedly the safest investment there is, lost a fifth or more of their value in a year.

@credit_risk Credit Risk
You collected your first coupon. On a company's bond, the next one isn't guaranteed.

Each company pays more than the Treasury to borrow: its credit spread. Steady Overworld Utility pays 0.01% a day more; Redstone Dynamics pays 0.30% more. That extra is your pay for the chance of not being paid.

The spread moves. When a company's earnings fall, lenders get nervous and want more, so its old bonds lose value long before anything goes wrong. After two losing quarters in a row a company defaults: its coupons stop, and each bond pays back only $40 of its $100. The company carries on, but its new bonds must pay far more.

So check the Company tab at the Stock Exchange before you lend. A high yield is often a warning, not a gift. Spreading your bonds across several issuers keeps one default from hurting much, and the Treasury never defaults.

Real world: agencies grade company bonds, and junk bonds pay high yields because some of them fail. In a bankruptcy, bondholders are paid before shareholders, which is why they often get something back.

@net_worth Net Worth
You set up a Records Terminal. For the first time you can see everything you own in one place, and what you owe.

Net worth is simple: everything you own at today's prices, minus your debts. Cash counts at face. Shares count at their last trade, bonds at what the Bond Desk would pay, goods at what the Dealer would pay. A loan counts against you until it's repaid.

The terminal only sees what's linked to it: your Bank Vault and the Safe Deposit Boxes and Trade Route Crates you connect with a Record Link. Papers stuffed in an ordinary chest are off the books. Organize your storage and your records get better.

The Holdings tab shows a gain or loss next to each paper, but only where the purchase is on record. Shares bought at the Stock Exchange and bonds bought at the Bond Desk have a known cost. Papers you picked up elsewhere show a question mark, because without a cost you can't tell a gain from a loss.

The Income tab adds up where your money came from each week: selling goods, interest, dividends, coupons, and gains on what you sold. Watch which line grows.

Real world: every company publishes a balance sheet (what it owns and owes) and an income statement (what it earned). Investors read both, and so do banks before they lend.
